Methodology and sources

What is real, what is fixed, what is calculated and what is AI-generated.

Where research comes from

Records were found by searching NASA’s Technical Reports Server (NTRS) and journal pages with a DOI. Each links to its original source. Challenge-brief facts (suppression with CO₂ and helium, lunar atmosphere targets) are labelled as background, not as library evidence.

How records are verified

Every record shows what was read: “Full text reviewed”, “Abstract reviewed”, or “Search excerpt only (unverified)”. Values come from those texts and nothing else. Missing values show “Not reported in retrieved source”. A person still needs to open the original to confirm important claims.

Evidence labels

Experimental observation, quantitative measurement, model prediction and authors’ interpretation come from the source. “IGNIS interpretation” marks text written by this project. Predictions are never shown as measurements.

Calculated by code

Dashboard counts, library search and filters, comparability statuses in the Comparison Lab, and the Explore prototype relevance score (see its “How scores are calculated” note) are computed by fixed rules in the page. The Comparison Lab compares reported text fields, not raw measurements.

Fixed, written content

The Science page, Insights, research-gap list, fallback answers and investigation profile scores were written by the developer from the sources and the challenge brief. They are not generated live.

AI-generated

Live assistant answers are generated by Google Gemini, using the library records and rules sent from the server. They can be wrong. Citations are checked against the library, and unknown ones are flagged. With no API key the assistant uses labelled fallback answers.

Experimental

The PDF checker extracts text from the first 5 pages in your browser. It does not validate or add anything to the library, and scanned PDFs without a text layer fail.

Not supported yet

No connection to the PSI database or raw measurement tables, no automatic extraction of findings from PDFs, no figure display, no partial-gravity data, and no numerical modeling.

Dataset limits

The library is a small, hand-selected sample of NTRS and journal records, not all NASA combustion research. Most records were read at abstract level. Absence of a topic here does not mean no research exists.

How IGNIS AI addresses the challenge

Flame in Freefall asks for tools that find, compare and interpret microgravity combustion research for fire safety in human spaceflight.

  • Discover: a searchable library of real NASA and journal records, each linked to its source.
  • Compare: a Comparison Lab that rates whether two studies can fairly be compared and refuses unsupported conclusions.
  • Interpret: insights and an AI assistant that separate observations, measurements, predictions, authors’ interpretations and IGNIS interpretations.
  • Prioritize: a transparent prototype relevance ranking for different habitats.

IGNIS AI is a research exploration and decision-support prototype. It is not an operational spacecraft fire-safety system, and NASA does not endorse it.

Known gap: partial gravity

The PSI records currently included in IGNIS do not contain partial-gravity tests. NASA's LUCI investigation has conducted combustion tests in simulated lunar gravity aboard a rotating sounding rocket. FM² is a separate investigation of material flammability on the lunar surface; its results are not included in the current IGNIS collection. E-FIELD Flames studies how electric fields affect flame ions and produce ion-driven airflow in microgravity; it does not simulate partial gravity.

Rank investigations by prototype relevance

Choose a habitat and set what matters. Scores are prototype relevance scores written by the IGNIS developer. They are not NASA ratings and are not evidence that anything is safe.

How scores are calculated

Each investigation profile has four editorial 0 to 5 estimates: crew-habitat relevance, support for predictive modeling, relevance to new safety equipment, and Earth applications. Your sliders set the weights. Score = weighted average of the four estimates, divided by 5, as a percentage. For habitats other than the spacecraft cabin, 0.8 points (of 5) are added to investigations flagged as high-oxygen relevant and 0.6 to those flagged as partial-gravity relevant; the total is capped at 100. The estimates are judgments, not measurements, and a high score does not mean the investigation proves a safety conclusion.

The science behind the flames

Flames without buoyancy

On Earth, hot gas rises and draws fresh air in, which makes a candle flame a flickering teardrop. In microgravity, buoyancy-driven convection is greatly reduced. Oxygen and heat are then carried by diffusion and by any forced flow, such as ventilation. In still air a flame can become rounder and bluer; with airflow it behaves differently.

Why oxygen matters, and why it is not enough

Flammability depends strongly on oxygen concentration. Per the challenge brief, spacesuits hold 100% oxygen at 4.3 psi, against 14.7 psi at sea level, and Moon habitats are expected at likely 30% oxygen or more at 10.2 psi or less. Oxygen alone cannot establish ignition risk: pressure, airflow, fuel properties, geometry and the ignition source all matter, and each experiment’s result belongs to its own conditions.

Airflow

Airflow matters in microgravity. Depending on speed and oxygen, flow can sustain a flame, change its shape, or help extinguish it. That is why test conditions record flow speed alongside oxygen. Library studies report results at specific speeds, from about 0.4 to 55 cm/s.

Three layers of fire safety

Prevention

Control ignition sources and choose materials using flammability limits. Studied in BASS, BASS-II and Saffire-I to -III.

Detection

Smoke behaves differently without gravity. SAME and SAME-R tested detection on the ISS.

Suppression

The challenge brief says FLEX diluted the chamber atmosphere with carbon dioxide and helium. In the IGNIS library, the evidence is limited to droplet tests (excerpt-level) and to Saffire extinguishment observations. See Insights.

About IGNIS AI

An independent research exploration and decision-support prototype built for the NASA Space Apps Challenge “Flame in Freefall: SI-Powered Fire Safety Insights from Microgravity Combustion Data”.

What it is

A tool to find, compare and interpret microgravity combustion studies, with clear labels for what each source actually reports.

What it is not

It is not a NASA product, is not endorsed by NASA, and is not an operational spacecraft fire-safety system. Do not use it to select materials or design hardware.

How rankings work

Explore shows prototype relevance scores from editorial 0–5 estimates and your slider weights. They are not NASA ratings and do not mean a study proves anything about safety.

The assistant’s limits

Answers are written by an AI model from the library records. It can be wrong. Check the cited sources and NASA’s PSI database.
